# How the Government Buys Now: Rules Knowledge Base

Checked against sources on November 27, 2026. Each entry has the same six fields: Change (old rule to new rule); Source (document and link); Status (effective date, and whether final, class deviation, proposed or statute); Affects (who it hits most, with dollar figures quoted from the source); Common mistake (what contractors get wrong, and what to do instead); How to use it (when to bring it up with a contractor, and what to say).

Entries marked [Not fully verified] rest on a secondary source or carry a detail that couldn't be confirmed in the official text. Check those before quoting them as law. Order: small business entries first (1 to 7), then the new buying paths (8 to 15), then outreach practice (16).

## PART A. Small business and set-asides

### 1. Agencies no longer have to consider the socioeconomic programs first
- Change: Old FAR 19.203(c) told contracting officers to "first consider" 8(a), HUBZone, SDVOSB and WOSB before a general small business set-aside. The rewritten Part 19 has no order of precedence, so the contracting officer chooses.
- Source: RFO model deviation text for FAR Part 19, adopted by each agency through a class deviation. Two examples: Department of War, DFARS Class Deviation 2026-O0037: https://www.acquisition.gov/sites/default/files/page_file_uploads/DoD_RFO_Deviation_Part-19.pdf ; VA, Class Deviation FAR Part 19 (Nov 19, 2025): https://department.va.gov/acquisition-and-logistics/wp-content/uploads/sites/58/2026/03/deviation-far-part-19.pdf
- Status: Class deviations, agency by agency. Department of War: effective Feb 1, 2026. VA: effective when signed, Nov 19, 2025, for "All VA procurements." The codified FAR Part 19 is unchanged. As of Nov 20, 2026, no Part 19 rule had been proposed. [Not fully verified: whether the Sept 18, 2026 batch of proposed rules includes Part 19.]
- Affects: 8(a), HUBZone, SDVOSB and WOSB firms that relied on getting first look at a requirement.
- Common mistake: Assuming a certification guarantees a set-aside. Instead, sell capability first, and give the contracting officer a reason to pick your program: market research responses showing two or more capable firms in your category.
- How to use it: Bring it up whenever a client leads with their certification. Say: "Your certification is a tool the contracting officer may use, not one they have to use. Show up in market research so the set-aside decision goes your way."

### 2. Rule of Two: one test above the micro-purchase threshold
- Change: The old FAR had separate tests below and above the simplified acquisition threshold. The rewritten 19.104-1 uses one test above the micro-purchase threshold: set aside when the contracting officer expects offers from two or more responsible small businesses that are competitive on fair market price, quality and delivery.
- Source: RFO Part 19 model deviation text, 19.104-1. The VA deviation memo says the rule is "required by statute" between the micro-purchase and simplified acquisition thresholds and was retained above the SAT.
- Status: Class deviations, as in entry 1. The basic contract-level Rule of Two remains and can still be protested.
- Affects: Every small business. The micro-purchase threshold is $15,000 and the SAT is $350,000 (entry 6).
- Common mistake: Not answering sources sought notices. The Rule of Two is decided from market research, so if fewer than two capable small firms respond, the work goes unrestricted. Answer every relevant sources sought notice with specific evidence of capability.
- How to use it: Raise it when a client asks why something wasn't set aside, or before any sources sought response. Say: "A set-aside happens when the contracting officer sees two capable small firms. Your response is one of the two."

### 3. Set-asides on orders under multiple-award contracts are discretionary
- Change: Under SBA's rules, setting aside orders under multiple-award contracts was already a "may." Two FAR Council proposals of Jan 15, 2025 would have required order-level set-asides and limited protests; both were withdrawn on June 12, 2025. The rewritten Part 19, at 19.111-2, reportedly declares order-level set-aside decisions discretionary and not protestable.
- Source: Withdrawal notice: https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/06/12/2025-10611/federal-acquisition-regulation-protests-of-orders-under-certain-multiple-award-contracts ; SBA's proposed order-level Rule of Two (Oct 25, 2024): https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-10-25/html/2024-24716.htm
- Status: The FAR proposals are withdrawn. 19.111-2 is class deviation text that never went through notice and comment, and courts haven't tested it. [Not fully verified: the "not protestable" wording comes from a secondary source, GovCon Forge (Nov 20, 2026).] SBA's Oct 25, 2024 proposal: final status not confirmed.
- Affects: Small businesses holding seats on vehicles such as GSA MAS, OASIS+ and agency IDIQs.
- Common mistake: Thinking a seat on a vehicle means a stream of set-aside orders. Instead, sell to the ordering offices directly. Each order is now an agency choice.
- How to use it: Raise it when a client holds a vehicle but gets few orders. Say: "The vehicle is a license to compete, not a pipeline. Go to the offices that order on it."

### 4. VA still has to buy from veteran-owned firms first
- Change: None. The VA's Veterans First Rule of Two comes from statute (38 U.S.C. 8127, upheld in Kingdomware Technologies v. United States, 2016), so a FAR deviation can't remove it. The VA adopted the rewritten Part 19 for everything else.
- Source: 38 U.S.C. 8127: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/38/8127 ; VA Part 19 deviation (link in entry 1)
- Status: Statute, in force.
- Affects: SDVOSBs and VOSBs gain. Other small businesses competing at the VA come second.
- Common mistake: Applying the weakened government-wide set-aside picture to the VA. At the VA, veteran-owned firms still come first. A non-veteran small business should ask how each buy will be set aside and plan to team with a veteran-owned firm when needed.
- How to use it: Raise it in every VA-facing report. Say: "Ask each VA buyer whether the work will be set aside for veteran-owned firms or for small businesses generally. That decides whether you bid as prime or team." [Not fully verified: VA's own SDVOSB and VOSB sole-source dollar limits were not checked for this file.]

### 5. 8(a) changes in the rewritten Part 19 [Not fully verified]
- Change: 19.108-7 adds a competition-first step: below the competitive threshold, the agency tries a competitive 8(a) order on an SBA-approved governmentwide contract before a sole-source award. 19.108-11 automatically releases a requirement from the 8(a) program when the follow-on will be set aside for HUBZone, SDVOSB or WOSB firms, ending "once 8(a), always 8(a)."
- Source: RFO Part 19 model deviation text. These details come from GovCon Forge (Nov 20, 2026), not the official text. The Department of War deviation separately says SBA may not accept a Department of War sole-source 8(a) contract that "exceeds $150 million" without a justification.
- Status: Class deviations.
- Affects: 8(a) firms, especially those counting on sole-source follow-ons.
- Common mistake: Counting on incumbent 8(a) work coming back as 8(a). Instead, plan for the follow-on to be competed, or set aside under another program, and prepare early.
- How to use it: Raise it in any report for an 8(a) firm, or a firm about to graduate. Say: "Your current 8(a) work may not return as 8(a). Treat every follow-on as a competition."

### 6. Thresholds raised for inflation, October 1, 2025
- Change (old → new): Micro-purchase $10,000 → $15,000. Simplified acquisition (SAT) $250,000 → $350,000. Commercial simplified procedures (FAR 13.500) $7.5 million → $9 million. Certified cost or pricing data (contracts after July 1, 2018) $2 million → $2.5 million. Subcontracting plan $750,000 → $900,000. Subcontracting plan, construction $1.5 million → $2 million. 8(a) sole-source justification $25 million → $30 million. 8(a), HUBZone, WOSB sole source $4.5 million ($7 million manufacturing) → $5.5 million ($8.5 million manufacturing). SDVOSB sole source $4 million ($7 million manufacturing) → $5 million ($8.5 million manufacturing). Davis-Bacon is not adjusted: the statute "specifically excludes" it.
- Source: FAR Case 2024-001, FAC 2025-06, 90 FR 41872 (Aug 27, 2025): https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-08-27/html/2025-16412.htm
- Status: Final rule, effective Oct 1, 2025.
- Affects: Everyone, most of all small businesses doing purchase-order work and sole-source awards.
- Common mistake: Quoting the old $10,000 and $250,000 figures. Use the new ones. A contracting officer can now buy up to $350,000 with simplified procedures, which is the size a first award usually is.
- How to use it: Use these figures whenever you size a first target or discuss sole-source awards. Say: "Awards under $350,000 use simplified procedures. That's your first-award lane."

### 7. Size status after mergers, and the proposed size standards
- Change (final rule): Since Jan 16, 2025, a firm must recertify its size within 30 days of a merger, acquisition or sale that changes control, and before year five of long contracts. On set-aside multiple-award contracts, a firm that becomes other than small through recertification loses eligibility for new orders (effective Jan 17, 2026). Change (proposed): On Aug 20, 2026, SBA proposed new size standards for "338 industry groups and industries."
- Source: SBA final rule, 2024-29393 (Dec 17, 2024): https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/12/17/2024-29393 ; Proposed size standards, comment extension: https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/09/24/2026-19546/small-business-size-standards (RIN 3245-AI67, docket SBA-2026-0199)
- Status: The recertification rule is final. The size standards are proposed only, and comments closed Nov 20, 2026.
- Affects: Small businesses planning a sale, investment or merger, and firms near their size standard.
- Common mistake: Treating the proposed size standards as current. Today's standards still apply, so check the current table.
- How to use it: Raise it when a client mentions selling the company, taking investment, or being near its size limit. Say: "Check before you close the deal. A sale can cost you set-aside orders on multiple-award contracts."

## PART B. The new buying paths

### 8. The FAR overhaul (RFO)
- Change: The FAR is being rewritten to keep only provisions "required by statute or essential to sound procurement." Agencies apply the new text through class deviations while formal rulemaking runs.
- Source: Executive Order 14275, "Restoring Common Sense to Federal Procurement," Apr 15, 2025, 90 FR 16447: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-04-18/html/2025-06839.htm . Model deviation text and agency deviations are on acquisition.gov.
- Status: Class deviations in effect by agency (Department of War's from Feb 1, 2026). Formal proposed rules have begun: four published June 23, 2026 (Parts 1, 2, 4, 33, 39, 40, 53, and Parts 3 and 49); four more Sept 18, 2026 covering 16 more parts, comments due Oct 19, 2026. None are final.
- Affects: Everyone. Small businesses feel it most through Part 19 (entries 1 to 3).
- Common mistake: Reading the old FAR on eCFR and assuming that's what the agency follows. Instead, check whether the buying agency has adopted the RFO deviation for that part (the adoption list is on acquisition.gov).
- How to use it: Mention it in every report's "how they buy now" section. Say: "Agencies are working from a shorter, rewritten rulebook. Ask the contracting officer which version applies."

### 9. Commercial first
- Change: Agencies must buy commercial products and services to the maximum extent practicable. A non-commercial buy needs a written justification with market research, approved in writing. At the Department of War, FY2026 NDAA Sec. 1822 requires a memorandum signed by the Portfolio Acquisition Executive before using non-commercial procedures.
- Source: Executive Order 14271, "Ensuring Commercial, Cost-Effective Solutions in Federal Contracts," Apr 15, 2025, 90 FR 16433: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-04-18/html/2025-06835.htm ; FY2026 NDAA, Pub. L. 119-60: https://www.congress.gov/119/plaws/publ60/PLAW-119publ60.htm
- Status: The EO is in effect. The NDAA is statute, enacted Dec 18, 2025.
- Affects: Firms that sell standard products and services.
- Common mistake: Pitching custom solutions. Instead, describe what you sell as a commercial product or service with commercial pricing.
- How to use it: Raise it in pricing and positioning sections. Say: "Present your work as a commercial service with published rates. Buyers now have to justify anything else."

### 10. Common buying moves to GSA
- Change: Common goods and services are being consolidated at GSA. Agencies must use an existing governmentwide vehicle instead of awarding a new contract, unless their agency head grants an exception (through a FAR 8.004 deviation).
- Source: Executive Order 14240, "Eliminating Waste and Saving Taxpayer Dollars by Consolidating Procurement," Mar 20, 2025: https://govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-03-25/html/2025-05197.htm ; OMB M-25-31, "Consolidating Federal Procurement Activities," Jul 18, 2025: https://shared.govexec.com/media/general/2025/7/m-25-31-consolidating-federal-procurement-activities.pdf
- Status: Both in effect. Category transfers are phased, with no single deadline in M-25-31.
- Affects: Firms selling IT, professional services, facilities, office supplies and other common categories.
- Common mistake: Chasing stand-alone agency contracts for common work. Get on the GSA vehicle that covers your category first (Schedule, OASIS+, or a Best in Class contract).
- How to use it: Raise it whenever the client sells a common service. Say: "Agencies are being told to use existing GSA vehicles. Without a GSA vehicle, you won't see much of that work."

### 11. Portfolio Acquisition Executives replace Program Executive Officers (Department of War)
- Change: The Defense Acquisition System became the Warfighting Acquisition System. Program Executive Officers are being replaced by Portfolio Acquisition Executives (PAEs): one leader per capability area, with contracting officers under them, "held solely accountable for outcomes," and speed as the main measure. The FY2026 NDAA codified the PAE role.
- Source: Memo "Transforming the Defense Acquisition System into the Warfighting Acquisition System to Accelerate Fielding of Urgently Needed Capabilities to Our Warriors," Nov 7, 2025, law-firm summary: https://www.insidegovernmentcontracts.com/2025/12/from-das-to-was-secretary-hegseths-acquisition-overhaul-and-what-it-means-for-industry/ (the memo itself is on war.gov); FY2026 NDAA Sec. 1802
- Status: Memo in effect; statute enacted Dec 18, 2025. The transition is to finish within two years of the memo. Services announced their PAEs through 2026.
- Affects: Anyone selling to the Department of War, especially firms with a specific capability.
- Common mistake: Still working through layers of program offices. Instead, find the PAE whose portfolio owns your capability and that portfolio's small business lead.
- How to use it: Required in every report for a client selling to the Department of War. Say: "One leader now owns your buyer's capability area, with contracting in the same office. That's fewer doors than the old structure."

### 12. Commercial Solutions Openings (CSOs)
- Change: A CSO lets the Department of War ask industry to propose solutions to a stated problem, then award from those proposals. FY2026 NDAA Sec. 1823 widened CSOs from new technology to all commercial products and services, and allows sole-source follow-on production contracts.
- Source: 10 U.S.C. 3458: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/3458 ; FY2026 NDAA Sec. 1823
- Status: Statute, in force.
- Affects: Commercial firms and small businesses with a product or service that solves a stated problem.
- Common mistake: Waiting for a regular solicitation. Instead, watch for open CSOs from the relevant portfolio and propose against the stated problem. A win can lead straight to production.
- How to use it: Raise it when a client has a distinct product or service and a Department of War buyer. Say: "This office takes proposals against a problem year-round. You don't wait for a request for proposals." [Not fully verified: CSO dollar limits were not checked.]

### 13. Other Transaction agreements (OTAs)
- Change: No new change confirmed in the FY2026 NDAA. OTAs remain the main path for prototypes, often awarded through consortia, and a successful prototype can lead to follow-on production without a new competition.
- Source: 10 U.S.C. 4021 (research): https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/4021 ; 10 U.S.C. 4022 (prototypes and follow-on production): https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/4022
- Status: Statute, in force. The Warfighting Acquisition System memo favors consortium-based OTAs for modular, competitive prototyping.
- Affects: Technology firms, nontraditional defense contractors and small businesses.
- Common mistake: Trying to win an OTA alone with no consortium membership. Instead, join the consortium tied to your capability area and team with members who already win prototypes.
- How to use it: Raise it for technology or product clients. Say: "Prototype work runs through consortia. Join the one for your area and team on the next project call."

### 14. Broad Agency Announcements (BAAs)
- Change: None confirmed. BAAs remain the way agencies buy basic and applied research, by accepting proposals against broad research topics.
- Source: FAR 35.016: https://www.acquisition.gov/far/35.016
- Status: In force. [Not fully verified: whether the rewritten Part 35 renumbers or changes 35.016.]
- Affects: Research-oriented firms and universities.
- Common mistake: Treating a BAA like a request for proposals. Instead, send a short white paper first and talk to the program manager before a full proposal.
- How to use it: Raise it only for research clients. Say: "Send a two-page white paper to the program manager before writing a full proposal."

### 15. Relief for small and nontraditional defense contractors (FY2026 NDAA)
- Change: Sec. 1826 exempts nontraditional defense contractors, including small businesses, from FAR Part 31 cost principles and certified cost or pricing data requirements (the Department of War may waive the exemption). Sec. 824 calls for guidance letting firms with little federal past performance submit other evidence. Sec. 1804 raises the Department of War's certified cost or pricing data threshold from $2 million to $10 million (before inflation adjustment) for contracts after June 30, 2026. Sec. 875 lets the Department of War withhold up to 5% of payments to an incumbent whose GAO protest is dismissed as baseless.
- Source: FY2026 NDAA, Pub. L. 119-60 (link in entry 9). Details are from the ABA summary: https://www.americanbar.org/groups/public_contract_law/resources/procurement-lawyer/2026-winter/national-defense-authorization-act-fiscal-year-2026/
- Status: Statute, enacted Dec 18, 2025. [Not fully verified: DFARS implementation status.]
- Affects: Small businesses and commercial firms new to the Department of War.
- Common mistake: Assuming you need government-style accounting before you can bid. Instead, ask whether the buy qualifies for the nontraditional exemptions, and use commercial or other past performance where the guidance allows it.
- How to use it: Raise it when a newcomer worries about compliance costs. Say: "Congress cut several accounting requirements for small and nontraditional firms selling to the Department of War."

## PART C. Outreach in a less set-aside world (GovCon Giants practice, not law)
### 16. How outreach changes
1. Set-asides now depend on market research. Answer every relevant sources sought notice and RFI with specific evidence (entries 1 to 2).
2. Vehicles are licenses, not pipelines. Go to the ordering offices on each vehicle you hold (entry 3), and get on GSA vehicles for common work (entry 10).
3. Find the owner, not the office. At the Department of War, find the PAE whose portfolio owns your capability, and its small business lead (entry 11).
4. Use the paths that don't wait for a solicitation. CSOs, OTA consortia and BAAs accept proposals against a stated problem (entries 12 to 14).
5. Lead with commercial terms. Commercial products and services at commercial prices are now the default (entry 9).
6. Prime with partners. With less set-aside protection, team with firms that already win: incumbents, holders of the vehicle, and veteran-owned firms at the VA (entry 4).
7. The pitch formula for every meeting: a buyer problem from a cited source, your proof against it, and one question about how the next buy will be structured.
