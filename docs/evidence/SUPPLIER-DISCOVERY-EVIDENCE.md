# Supplier Discovery — Evidence Library (case file)

**Purpose.** The marshaled body of evidence behind Mindy / GovCon Giants' Supplier
Discovery thesis: that under-competed public solicitations cost governments money,
that the #1 reason firms don't bid is they never heard, that fragmentation + paywalled
access suppress competition, and that a shrinking/concentrated supplier base is a real
risk. Built like a journalist's case file — **have the data ready when they come asking.**

**Rules of this file (non-negotiable).**
- Every entry is a REAL, verifiable source. No LLM-fabricated citations, ever.
- Each entry carries a **verification status**, confirmed by opening the source page:
  - ✅ **CONFIRMED** — the exact finding was read directly off the source (landing page or PDF).
  - 🟡 **LEAD** — the source exists and bibliographic details are confirmed, but the specific
    finding could not be read off the page (paywall / nav-only / captured from a search summary).
    Chase down / open the PDF before printing the number in a government-facing document.
- **NEW to this file** = deliberately excludes what our Institute white papers already cite
  (Liscow/Nober/Slattery, Open Contracting Partnership, Census of Governments, our existing
  GAO reports, Miller3/Raleigh, BBC/Boston, Ramsey/aggregators, Portland/OCDS, Harvard GPL /
  St. Paul, AASHTO/FHWA, Bipartisan Policy Center, CSIS, SBA Scorecard, OMB M-23-11, 23 CFR/USC).
  This library is additive — it broadens the case.
- Organized by **claim**, not by document, so any claim can be defended on demand.

## Claim A — More competition / more bidders lowers what governments pay; and widely advertising a solicitation causally increases bidders

### ✅ CONFIRMED — Coviello & Mariniello (2014), *Journal of Public Economics*
- **Cite:** Decio Coviello & Mario Mariniello, "Publicity requirements in public procurement:
  Evidence from a regression discontinuity design," *Journal of Public Economics*, vol. 109
  (2014), pp. 76–100. DOI 10.1016/j.jpubeco.2013.10.008.
- **URL:** https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/pubeco/v109y2014icp76-100.html
- **Finding:** A stricter publicity/advertising requirement **causally induced more entry**
  (more bidders), and the number of bidders is the **channel** through which added publicity
  raised winning rebates — i.e., **lowered the price the government paid.**
- **Why it matters to us:** This is the cleanest causal evidence that *advertising a
  solicitation more widely lowers procurement cost* — the exact mechanism of Supplier Discovery.
- **Scope/caveat:** Italian public-works auctions; regression-discontinuity at a publication
  threshold (rigorous causal design), but one country / one contract type. Also shifts wins
  toward larger/out-of-region firms.
- **Verification:** Opened the RePEc abstract page directly; authors, venue, vol/year/pages,
  RD design, Italy, and the entry→rebate mechanism all read off the source (2026-08-07).

### ✅ CONFIRMED — Hong & Shum (2002), *Review of Economic Studies* — the honest counter-nuance
- **Cite:** Han Hong & Matthew Shum, "Increasing Competition and the Winner's Curse: Evidence
  from Procurement," *The Review of Economic Studies*, vol. 69, no. 4 (2002), pp. 871–898.
- **URL:** https://academic.oup.com/restud/article/69/4/871/1551658
  (working-paper PDF: http://www.its.caltech.edu/~mshum/papers/winner.pdf)
- **Finding:** In **common-value** procurement settings, procurement costs can **rise** as the
  number of bidders increases, because the winner's curse makes bidders bid more conservatively
  as competitors are added. Uses **Florida highway construction** procurement data.
- **Why it matters to us:** We cite this *against ourselves* — the intellectually honest
  boundary on "more bidders is better." Reinforces our real claim: the gains are strongest
  moving a solicitation from **1–2 bidders to 4–5**, not chasing an unlimited bidder count.
  A skeptical official who knows auction theory will respect that we flagged it.
- **Scope/caveat:** Common-value environments specifically; does NOT overturn the general
  private-value result that more bidders lower price. Cite as nuance, not headline.
- **Verification:** Bibliographic details confirmed on the Oxford Academic landing page; the
  finding text confirmed by opening the author's Caltech working-paper PDF directly (2026-08-07).

### 🟡 LEAD — Gupta (2002), *Atlantic Economic Journal* — US, dual-purpose (savings + threshold)
- **Cite:** Srabana Gupta, "Competition and Collusion in a Government Procurement Auction
  Market," *Atlantic Economic Journal*, vol. 30, no. 1 (2002), pp. 13–25. DOI 10.1007/BF02299143.
- **URL:** https://go.gale.com/ps/i.do?p=AONE&id=GALE%7CA85251735 (paywalled → auth redirect)
- **Reported finding (to confirm):** Winning bids fall as bidders rise up to ~**6–8 firms**,
  after which additional bidders no longer move the winning price (market fully competitive at
  ~8). **Florida highway-construction** auctions.
- **Why it matters:** A single U.S. paper delivering BOTH "more bidders → lower price" AND the
  diminishing-returns threshold — very useful if confirmed.
- **Status:** Bibliographic details consistent across indexes; the FINDING was not read off the
  source (Gale redirects to an auth gate). **Open the article via a library/JSTOR copy before
  citing the 6–8 threshold in any government-facing document.**

### 🟡 LEAD — Iimi (2007), *Journal of International Development* — per-bidder elasticity
- **Cite:** Atsushi Iimi, "Aid and Competition in Procurement Auctions: A Case of Highway
  Projects," *Journal of International Development*, vol. 19, no. 7 (2007), pp. 891–907.
  DOI 10.1002/jid.1379.
- **URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/jid.1379 (abstract paywalled, HTTP 402)
- **Reported finding (to confirm):** ~1% more bidders → ~**0.2% lower** equilibrium bid.
- **Scope/caveat:** Donor-financed (ADB) highway projects — development-aid context, one class.
- **Status:** Title/author/venue confirmed; the **0.2% elasticity was captured from a search
  excerpt, not read off the source.** Open the PDF before printing the figure.

### 🟡 LEAD — Tas (2020), *International Tax and Public Finance* — cost-minimizing bidder counts
- **Cite:** Bedri Kamil Onur Tas, "Optimal Bidder Participation in Public Procurement Auctions,"
  *International Tax and Public Finance*, vol. 27 (2020), pp. 987–1032. DOI 10.1007/s10797-018-9515-2.
- **URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10797-018-9515-2 (auth gate)
- **Reported finding (to confirm):** Number of bidders significantly lowers procurement price,

- **Status:** Bibliographic details confirmed; the **8/7/13 thresholds came from two independent
  search summaries, not the source page.** Verify against the article before citing verbatim.

### 🟡 LEAD — Kenny & Crisman (2016), Center for Global Development WP 437
- **Cite:** Charles Kenny & Ben Crisman, "Results Through Transparency: Does Publicity Lead to
  Better Procurement?," Center for Global Development, Working Paper 437 (2016).
- **URL:** https://ideas.repec.org/p/cgd/wpaper/437.html

## Claim B — Firms don't bid mainly because they never learned the opportunity existed (awareness / notice failure); reaching them is a distribution problem

### ✅ CONFIRMED — World Bank WP 10390, Blum et al. (2023) — findability → measurably more bidders AND lower prices
- **Cite:** Jürgen René Blum, Arkopal Datta, Mihály Fazekas, Sushmita Samaddar & Ishtiak
  Siddique, "Introducing E-Procurement in Bangladesh: The Promise of Efficiency and Openness,"
  World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 10390 (2023).
- **URL:** https://www.govtransparency.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/e-procurement_bangladesh_wp_WB_April2023.pdf
- **Finding (verbatim from the abstract):** "the number of **bidders increasing by 1.6–2.2** and
  the **probability of a single bidder decreasing by 7.8–13.5 percentage points** … discounts
  firms offer increase by **7.4–8.0 percentage points**." I.e., moving tenders online (making
  opportunities findable) both widened the bidder pool and lowered price.
- **Why it matters to us:** THE cornerstone number for "make it findable → more competition."
  It gives a bidder-count lift AND a price effect from the exact intervention we run.
- **Scope/caveat:** Bangladesh, 2011–16, fixed-effects on matched contracts; developing-country
  e-GP — external validity to US local government is directional, not literal.
- **Verification:** ✅ **Independently confirmed — I extracted the abstract text from the primary
  PDF myself** and read the 1.6–2.2 / 7.8–13.5pp / 7.4–8.0pp figures + WP number 10390 + authors
  directly (2026-08-07). (Highest-confidence entry.)

### ✅ CONFIRMED — OECD (2018), *SMEs in Public Procurement* — information access is a top-ranked barrier

## Claim C — Fragmentation + paywalled access suppress competition; open / e-procurement raises it

### ✅ CONFIRMED — Bandiera, Prat & Valletti (2009), *American Economic Review* — the "passive waste" cornerstone
- **Cite:** Oriana Bandiera, Andrea Prat & Tommaso Valletti, "Active and Passive Waste in
  Government Spending: Evidence from a Policy Experiment," *American Economic Review*, vol. 99,
  no. 4 (Sept. 2009), pp. 1278–1308.
- **URL:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.99.4.1278
- **Finding:** **Passive waste accounts for 83% of total estimated waste** in government
  spending — i.e., most overspending is inefficiency / failure to shop the market, **not**
  corruption ("active waste"). Data: purchases of standardized goods by Italian public bodies,
  exploiting a national procurement-agency policy experiment.
- **Why it matters to us:** The single most powerful frame in the whole file — governments
  overpay mostly because buyers don't reach the better-priced option, exactly the access problem
  Supplier Discovery attacks. And it disarms the "this is really about corruption" objection.
- **Scope/caveat:** Standardized goods, Italian public bodies; a top-journal quasi-experiment.
- **Verification:** Opened the AEA article page; authors, AER vol 99/4/2009/pp.1278–1308, the 83%
  passive-waste figure, and the active/passive definitions all read off the source (2026-08-07).

### ✅ CONFIRMED — Lewis-Faupel, Neggers, Olken & Pande (2016), *AEJ: Economic Policy*
- **Cite:** Sean Lewis-Faupel, Yusuf Neggers, Benjamin A. Olken & Rohini Pande, "Can Electronic
  Procurement Improve Infrastructure Provision? Evidence from Public Works in India and
  Indonesia," *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy*, vol. 8, no. 3 (Aug. 2016), pp. 258–283.
- **URL:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20140258
- **Finding:** E-procurement showed **no price reduction**, but **broadened entry** — regions with
  e-procurement were more likely to have winners from **outside the local region**, facilitating
  entry from **higher-quality contractors** (better road quality in India, fewer delays in
  Indonesia).
- **Why it matters to us:** Evidence that making opportunities findable pulls in bidders beyond
  the usual local pool — the core of our "reach the firms two counties over" pitch. And an honest
  one: the benefit here shows up as *entry + quality*, not price.
- **Scope/caveat:** Public-works contracts in two developing countries; no measured price effect.
- **Verification:** Opened the AEA article page; authors, venue, vol 8/3/2016, and the no-price /
  out-of-region-entry / quality findings all read off the source (2026-08-07).

### ✅ CONFIRMED (direction) / 🟡 (magnitude) — Duguay, Rauter & Samuels (2023), *Journal of Accounting Research*
- **Cite:** Raphael Duguay, Thomas Rauter & Delphine Samuels, "The Impact of Open Data on Public
  Procurement," *Journal of Accounting Research*, vol. 61, no. 4 (2023), pp. 1159–1224.
  DOI 10.1111/1475-679X.12479.
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.1111/1475-679X.12479
  (author summary: https://www.insead.edu/faculty-research/publications/journal-articles/impact-open-data-public-procurement)
- **Finding (direction ✅):** After the EU made historical procurement notices freely available
  for bulk download in one user-friendly database, officials became significantly **more likely
  to award contracts through open competitive bidding** (vs. restricted/negotiated).
- **Magnitude (🟡 to confirm):** The often-quoted "~12% more bids per tender / ~8% lower prices"
  figures are from the paywalled body — **verify in full text before printing.**
- **Scope/caveat:** Difference-in-differences around EU/TED open-data publication thresholds.
- **Verification:** Citation + the open-bidding direction confirmed off the INSEAD author page +
  RePEc abstract; the 12%/8% percentages NOT directly verified (Wiley paywall).

### 🟡 LEAD — Kochanova, Hasnain & Larson (2020), *World Bank Economic Review* — the honest conditional
- **Cite:** Aleksandra Kochanova, Zahid Hasnain & Bradley Larson, "Does E-Government Improve
  Government Capacity? Evidence from Tax Compliance Costs, Tax Revenue, and Public Procurement

## Claim D — A shrinking / concentrated government supplier base is a documented cost-and-risk problem; widening the pool is a resilience strategy

> ### ⚠️ FRAMING GUARDRAIL — READ BEFORE USING ANY SOURCE IN THIS SECTION (Eric, 2026-08-07)
> **These sources are about the HARD case, which is NOT our case.** The DoD "51→5 primes," the
> single-source GAO reports, and the sole-source concentration story are largely about markets
> where only one vendor *can* do the work — **jet engines, satellites, missile systems.** You
> **cannot** add bidders to those by publishing a notice; there is no market to reach.
>
> **Mindy's story is the opposite and must stay the opposite:** categories where a **real,
> competitive supplier base already exists but the solicitation isn't reaching it** — janitorial,
> landscaping, IT services, construction trades, professional services, commodity goods.
> **Under-SERVED, not under-SUPPLIED.**
>
> **How to use Claim D correctly:** ONLY as *contrast* and as proof that governments already treat
> supplier concentration as a named cost-and-risk problem. Frame it as: *"the government's own
> auditors flag concentration as a risk even where the market is genuinely thin — our work is the
> easier, addressable half: the categories that have capable firms who simply never heard."*
> **Never** cite these to imply we add competition to genuine sole-source buys. Our
> vendor-dependency capability finds **underserved-but-supplied** markets (capable firms exist,
> aren't bidding), not sole-source risk on jet engines.

_(All three GAO reports below are DISTINCT from GAO-22-104621, which our Institute papers already

## The size of the prize — "what if every U.S. city solved this"

**The honest rule for this section:** exactly ONE input is a hard measured number (Census total
expenditure). Everything else is a sourced *band*, not a point. We publish a **range**, label each
input green (measured) or yellow (assumption), apply the savings rate ONLY to the under-competed
slice (the #1 way these estimates get inflated), and name the missing dataset as part of the story.
**No single fabricated headline.**

> ### ⚠️ CONTESTABLE-MARKETS GUARDRAIL (Eric, 2026-08-07) — the load-bearing scoping rule
> The ~40% single-bid figures include a chunk of **genuine sole-source** buys — jet engines,
> proprietary systems, one-vendor markets — that **cannot** be fixed by getting the notice in
> front of more firms. **Those are NOT our market and must be netted OUT of the model.** Mindy's
> addressable slice is **under-SERVED, not under-SUPPLIED**: single-/low-bid solicitations in
> categories where a real competitive supplier base exists (janitorial, landscaping, IT services,
> construction trades, professional services, commodity goods) and simply isn't hearing about the
> opportunity. When we publish a number, the "share under-competed" input must be the
> **contestable** remainder, and the framing must be "where a market exists and isn't being
> reached" — never "add bidders to sole-source." Overstating this is the fastest way a skeptical
> official discredits the whole case.

### The model
```
ANNUAL SAVINGS  ≈  [ Addressable state & local procurement $ ]
                 ×  [ Share of that $ flowing through under-competed (1–2 bid) solicitations ]
                 ×  [ Cost reduction from moving those to real competition ]
```

### The inputs
| Variable | Value / range | Source(s) | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Total S&L expenditure** | **$3.7T** direct general expenditure, FY2021 ($1.8T state + $1.9T local) | ✅ Census / Urban Institute (below) | 🟢 **MEASURED (hard)** |
| **Procurement as a share of that** | **~20–33%** → addressable procurement ≈ **$0.9T–$1.5T** | 🟡 Manhattan Institute (~"$1 of every $4"); Deltek/McKinsey (~$1.5T SLED, industry-composite) | 🟡 **ASSUMPTION band** (biggest uncertainty) |
| **Share under-competed AND contestable** | **~40% single-bid, LESS the genuinely sole-source share** → plan on the **addressable remainder only** (sensitivity 30–45% single-bid × the contestable fraction) | 🟡 US federal 44% (Kang & Miller, LEAD) + EU >40% (EC/ECA) — analogues, no US-local primary data | 🟡 **ASSUMPTION (analogue) — must net out true sole-source (see guardrail below)** |
| **Savings when de-monopolized** | Conservative **~2–3% of spend** / ~1% per bidder · Central **~13.5%** on affected contracts · Optimistic **~8.3% per added bidder** / **20%+** vs. collusive | ✅/🟡 studies below (A–F) | 🟢 **range each end sourced** |

### Illustrative range (shown as a RANGE, never a point)
Applying the savings rate ONLY to the under-competed AND **contestable** slice — i.e. single-bid
share × the fraction that is a real reachable market, NOT genuine sole-source (guardrail above).
Plan on **~⅔ of single-bid buys being contestable** (conservative — treats a third as truly
sole-source):
- **Conservative:** $0.9T proc × (30% single-bid × 60% contestable = 18%) × ~3% savings ≈ **~$5B/yr**
- **Midpoint:** $1.2T × (40% × 67% = ~27%) × ~8% ≈ **~$26B/yr**
- **Optimistic:** $1.5T × (45% × 75% = ~34%) × ~13.5% ≈ **~$68B/yr**

> These three lines are **arithmetic on the sourced inputs above**, not measured outcomes. Two of
> three inputs are assumption bands, and the **contestable fraction is itself an assumption** (we
> have no US-local measure of how much single-bid spend is genuinely sole-source vs. merely
> unreached — flag it yellow). Present the method and the range; never quote a single number as
> "the" figure. The honest takeaway: **even after netting out sole-source, the reachable prize is
> billions per year.** ⚠️ Recompute if you change any band; this is the current illustration, not a
> stored result — and NEVER drop the contestable haircut (that's the jet-engine error).

### The data gap IS part of the pitch
There is **no clean, primary, published distribution of bid counts for U.S. municipal/local
solicitations.** The best proxies (US federal single-bid ~44%; EU single-bid >40%) both land near
~40% — a defensible planning assumption, **but state it as borrowed from federal + EU analogues,
not measured on US local data.** That missing dataset is exactly what a tool like Mindy begins to
produce — the absence is a reason the product should exist, and a reason to publish what we learn.
